What Is Codependency?

Codependency is a pattern where your sense of identity, worth, or stability becomes so tied to another person's needs, moods, or approval that your own needs quietly stop counting. The APA Dictionary of Psychology describes it broadly as a dysfunctional pattern in a relationship where one person's needs are subordinated to accommodate the needs of the other. Cleveland Clinic frames it as an imbalance where the "give" side of a relationship consistently outweighs the "take" — not once, but as the ongoing default.

Codependency doesn't require a partner who's abusive or even unkind. It can form around someone who's simply struggling — with addiction, illness, a mental health condition, or just a demanding personality — where one person quietly reorganizes their whole life around managing the other's problems.

Where Codependent Patterns Come From

Often a Survival Strategy, Not a Character Flaw

Codependent patterns frequently start early — in a household where a child had to manage a parent's addiction, mood, or crisis to keep things stable. Being hyper-attuned to someone else's needs, and quiet about your own, can be an adaptive skill in that environment. The problem is that the strategy doesn't automatically turn off once the environment changes.

It can also develop later in life, in a relationship where caretaking became the price of connection — where the relationship only felt secure when you were needed, so being needed slowly replaced being known.

Signs of Codependency

  • Your mood rises and falls with theirs, almost minute to minute, regardless of what's actually happening in your own life.
  • You find it genuinely hard to say no, even to requests that cost you real time, money, or energy you don't have to spare.
  • You've lost touch with your own preferences because you've spent so long deferring to theirs that you're not sure what you'd choose on your own.
  • You take responsibility for their feelings and choices, as if their bad day or bad decision is something you failed to prevent.
  • Friendships and hobbies you used to have have quietly disappeared, absorbed into managing this one relationship.
  • You feel a flash of anxiety, not relief, at the idea of them being fine without you. Being needed has become part of how you feel safe.
  • You over-explain or apologize for having needs at all, as if wanting something is an imposition.

How to Build Healthier Bonds

  1. Practice naming a preference out loud, even a small one — what you want for dinner, what you'd rather do this weekend. It's a muscle that atrophies fast and rebuilds slowly.
  2. Let a "no" stand without over-explaining it. A full paragraph of justification is often a sign the boundary itself still feels illegitimate to you.
  3. Separate empathy from responsibility. You can care deeply about someone's struggle without believing it's yours to fix.
  4. Rebuild something that's only yours — a friendship, a hobby, a routine that doesn't run through the other person at all.
  5. Get support built for this specifically. Codependency patterns are well studied, and a therapist familiar with them can help far faster than willpower alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is codependency the same as being a caring or generous person?

No. Caring and generosity leave room for your own needs to still matter. Codependency specifically describes a pattern where they consistently don't.

Can codependency happen in a friendship, not just romance?

Yes — the pattern of one person's needs consistently outweighing the other's can show up in friendships, family relationships, and romantic relationships alike.

Is codependency a diagnosis?

It's a descriptive term, not a formal clinical diagnosis on its own, though it's widely used in therapy and addiction recovery contexts to describe a real and workable pattern.

How is codependency different from a trauma bond?

A trauma bond specifically requires a cycle of real harm followed by intense reconciliation. Codependency doesn't require harm — it can form around caretaking a partner who is simply struggling, not mistreating you. Read more in our guide to what a trauma bond actually is.
