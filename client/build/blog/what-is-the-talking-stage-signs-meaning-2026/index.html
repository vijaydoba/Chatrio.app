What Is the Talking Stage? Signs, Meaning & How to Move Past It (2026)

What Is the Talking Stage?

The talking stage is the undefined period between meeting someone with romantic potential and actually calling it a relationship — daily texting, maybe a date or two, real interest on both sides, but no conversation about exclusivity, no label, and no agreement on where it's headed. A psychologist writing in Psychology Today describes it as the phase where two people are testing the hypothesis of a long-term connection on their own terms, whether that means texting only, occasional dates, or something more physical mixed in.

It isn't new — every generation has had some version of "seeing each other" before it's official — but the talking stage got its own name and its own anxieties once dating moved onto phones. A near-constant stream of messages can feel like intimacy without either person having agreed to anything, which is exactly what makes it so easy to read differently than the other person does.

Talking Stage vs Situationship vs Officially Dating

These three get used almost interchangeably, but they describe different amounts of time spent in ambiguity — and different reasons for it.

Talking stageSituationshipOfficially dating
Typical lengthDays to a few weeksMonths, sometimes longerOngoing, by mutual agreement
ExclusivityNot yet discussedOften assumed, rarely confirmedExplicitly agreed
PlansVague ("we should hang out")Regular but undefinedConcrete, forward-looking
The word "we"AvoidedAvoided despite the patternUsed freely

The difference that matters most: a talking stage is supposed to resolve one way or the other fairly quickly. A situationship is what happens when that resolution keeps getting avoided — the ambiguity itself becomes the relationship, sometimes for months.

Signs You're in the Talking Stage

  • You're texting constantly but have had few, if any, real dates — or the reverse: a couple of good dates but no idea what happens next.
  • Neither of you has used "boyfriend," "girlfriend," or even "seeing each other." You've both been careful to avoid it.
  • You still feel slightly odd mentioning them by name to close friends, because you're not sure what to call what this is.
  • Plans stay vague — "we should hang out sometime" instead of an actual day and time.
  • You're both still active on dating apps, and neither of you has asked the other to stop or brought it up.
  • One unanswered text can spiral into overthinking precisely because nothing about the connection is defined yet.
  • You've caught yourself Googling some version of "are we dating or just talking."

Why the Ambiguity Feels So Hard

The Uncertainty Isn't a Flaw — It's the Point

Research on how young people navigate this phase, published on The Conversation, frames the talking stage as functioning like a low-stakes vetting period: it lets two people assess compatibility while minimizing emotional risk, precisely because nothing has been agreed to yet. The discomfort isn't a design flaw — it's the tradeoff for being able to walk away without an official breakup.

That tradeoff doesn't make the uncertainty pleasant. Humans generally dislike ambiguous situations more than clearly bad ones, because an undefined connection can't be planned around — you can't grieve it, invest in it, or relax into it. A Forbes piece on the phenomenon points out that people often stay in it longer than they'd like simply because naming the ambiguity out loud feels riskier than tolerating it.

How to Move It Forward (or Let It Go)

  1. Say the quiet part out loud. One direct question — "what are we doing here?" — resolves more than weeks of hinting and hoping the other person brings it up first.
  2. Watch actions over two or three weeks, not just words. Consistent effort is a better signal than any one enthusiastic text.
  3. Give yourself an actual deadline. Open-ended ambiguity can drift indefinitely unless someone decides how long is reasonable.
  4. Get a real read through an actual conversation, not just texting. A call or a proper date surfaces things a text thread can hide — see our guide to making a video date actually work.
  5. If it's not moving, let it end without turning it into a whole thing. Nothing was official, so nothing has to be dramatically undone — just stop investing in it.

Some of the ambiguity of the talking stage comes from not knowing whether the other person is even looking for the same thing you are. Frequently Asked Questions

How long should the talking stage last?

There's no fixed timeline, but a few weeks is a reasonable point to check in about where things stand. If it's stretched into months with no conversation about it, that's closer to a situationship than a talking stage.

Is it normal to talk to more than one person during the talking stage?

Yes, and it's generally accepted as fair game until exclusivity is actually discussed — which is exactly why naming that conversation matters once things feel serious to either person.

What's the real difference between the talking stage and a situationship?

Duration and intent. The talking stage is meant to be a short vetting period that resolves into either dating or moving on. A situationship is what forms when that resolution keeps getting avoided and the ambiguity becomes the ongoing pattern.

How do you ask "what are we" without it feeling like an ultimatum?

Frame it as curiosity, not a demand — "I've really enjoyed this and I'm curious where your head's at" invites an honest answer more than "we need to talk." Tone and timing matter more than the exact words.
