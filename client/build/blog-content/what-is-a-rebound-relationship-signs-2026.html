What Is a Rebound Relationship?

A rebound relationship is a new romantic connection that starts shortly after a breakup, often before the previous relationship has been fully processed. The popular assumption is that it's inherently a mistake — a distraction dressed up as a new relationship, doomed by the unfinished business it's standing on. The research on this is more interesting, and less pessimistic, than the reputation suggests.

What the Research Actually Found

📊 Rebounding Was Linked to Better Outcomes, Not Worse

A study by psychologists Claudia Brumbaugh and R. Chris Fraley followed people after a breakup and compared those who started dating someone new sooner against those who waited. People in new relationships reported greater confidence in their own desirability and more resolution around their ex-partner than those who hadn't moved on to someone new — and among people who did start a new relationship, a faster start was associated with greater psychological and relational health, not less.

Source: Brumbaugh & Fraley, "Too fast, too soon? An empirical investigation into rebound relationships," Journal of Social and Personal Relationships (2015).

That doesn't mean speed is automatically good, or that everyone benefits equally. A separate line of research on attachment and breakups, published via PMC, found that how someone processes a breakup — how much they ruminate, how distressed they stay — shapes both their tendency to rebound and what they get out of it. In other words: the timing matters less on its own than what a person is actually bringing into the new connection.

Signs You (or They) Might Be Rebounding

  • The ex still comes up constantly — in comparisons, in stories, in a way that suggests the new connection is partly still about them.
  • The pace feels driven by not wanting to be alone, more than genuine curiosity about this specific person.
  • Any bad moment gets read through the lens of the last relationship, as either "this is different, thank god" or "here we go again."
  • There's real pressure to move fast — meeting friends, using serious labels — that outpaces how well you actually know each other.
  • You (or they) haven't spent meaningful time single between the breakup and this connection starting.

None of these prove a relationship is doomed on their own. They're worth noticing, because a new relationship is a harder place to process old grief than being on your own is — not because the timing itself is wrong.

Is a Rebound Relationship Always a Bad Idea?

No — and treating "rebound" as an automatic insult tends to miss the more useful question, which is what the new relationship is actually built on. A connection that starts soon after a breakup but is genuinely about the new person — their actual personality, actual compatibility, actual chemistry — isn't disqualified by its timing. A connection that's mostly functioning as a distraction, a way to prove something to an ex, or a replacement for time alone is more fragile, regardless of how much time has passed since the breakup.

The More Useful Question Than "Too Soon?"

Instead of asking how much time has passed, ask what the new relationship would look like if the breakup had never happened — would you still be drawn to this specific person, for their own reasons? If the honest answer is yes, timing alone isn't the problem.

If You're Considering Dating Again Soon

  1. Give yourself an honest, private check-in about whether you're excited about this person or relieved to not be alone — both can be true, but it's worth knowing which is doing more of the driving.
  2. Let the pace be set by how well you actually know them, not by how much comfort you need right now.
  3. Notice if you're using them to process the old relationship out loud. A little is normal; if it's constant, that's a sign the grief needs its own space.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should you wait before dating again after a breakup?

There's no universal timeline supported by research — what matters more is whether you're bringing genuine curiosity about the new person, rather than mainly using the relationship to avoid processing the last one.

Are rebound relationships doomed to fail?

Not according to the research. Studies have found people in new relationships after a breakup reported greater confidence and more resolution about their ex than those who stayed single — timing alone doesn't determine the outcome.

How can I tell if I'm using someone as a rebound?

Ask whether your interest in them would exist independent of the breakup — if you're drawn to who they specifically are, that's a good sign. If the main draw is not being alone or not thinking about your ex, that's worth being honest with yourself about.

Is it fair to date someone if you know you're on the rebound?

It can be, as long as you're honest about where you are rather than presenting yourself as fully ready when you're not. Clear expectations matter more than the calendar.
